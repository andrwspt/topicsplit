What is a semantic text splitter?

A semantic text splitter is a tool that breaks long text into segments based on meaning, not arbitrary word counts. Unlike naive splitters that chop every N words, a semantic splitter reads the text and finds where topics naturally shift — the same words clustering together signal a single topic, while a drop in shared vocabulary signals a boundary.

This matters because human language doesn't have uniform paragraph lengths. A single topic might span three sentences or thirty. Word-count splitters butcher semantic units, splitting mid-thought. Semantic splitters respect the natural structure of the text.

Why split text by meaning?

If you work with long-form content — articles, transcripts, meeting notes, research papers — you've faced the same problem: how to break a wall of text into clean, usable chunks.

For PKM and note-taking

Tools like Obsidian, Logseq, and Notion work best with atomic notes — small, self-contained pieces of information. A 5,000-word article doesn't fit neatly into one note. You need to split it into 5-10 topic segments, each one a clean atomic note.

Manual splitting is tedious and subjective. You read, you decide, you cut. A semantic splitter does the mechanical part — finding topic boundaries — so you can focus on deciding what to keep.

For researchers and students

Interview transcripts, lecture notes, and research papers are full of topic shifts. A semantic splitter lets you segment them automatically, making it easier to code, annotate, or summarize each section.

For writers

Long-form writers use segmentation to structure drafts, prepare content for serialization, or break blog posts into newsletter issues. Semantic splitting respects the natural flow of the writing.

How TopicSplit works

TopicSplit uses lexical cohesion analysis — a well-established NLP technique dating back to the 1990s (Hearst's TextTiling algorithm). Here's the simple version:

  1. Tokenize the text into sentences and content words (removing stop words like "the", "and", "of").
  2. Compute overlap between consecutive sentences — how many content words do they share?
  3. Find breakpoints — a sharp drop in overlap signals a topic shift.
  4. Group sentences between breakpoints into coherent segments.

No machine learning model. No API call. No server. Just ~130 lines of pure JavaScript running entirely in your browser.

// Simplified version of the algorithm
function overlap(sentenceA, sentenceB) {
  const wordsA = contentWords(sentenceA);
  const wordsB = contentWords(sentenceB);
  const shared = intersection(wordsA, wordsB).length;
  return shared / sqrt(wordsA.size * wordsB.size);
}

Frequently asked questions

What is semantic text splitting?

Semantic text splitting is the process of dividing a document into segments based on shifts in meaning, rather than arbitrary word counts. It uses lexical cohesion — the degree to which adjacent sentences share vocabulary — to identify natural topic boundaries.

Is this different from AI summarization?

Yes. AI summarizers rewrite your text — they condense, paraphrase, or generate new text. TopicSplit preserves your exact words. It only finds where to cut, making it ideal for atomic notes where you want the original phrasing.

Does my data leave my browser?

No. TopicSplit runs entirely in your browser. There is no server, no API key, no network call. Your text never leaves your device.

Can I use the splits commercially?

Yes. TopicSplit is MIT licensed. The output is yours to use however you want — in notes, papers, newsletters, commercial content.

What languages does it support?

TopicSplit works with English text. The stopword list is English-specific, but the lexical cohesion algorithm is language-agnostic. Non-English text will still produce reasonable splits, though less precise than English.

Yes! TopicSplit Pro is a command-line tool that splits entire folders of articles in one command. Same algorithm, same privacy — just for power users.
